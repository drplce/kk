I Origins

Kobus

An Afrikaans given name, the short form of Jakobus, which is the Dutch form of Jacob. It sits in the same family as Jacques, Giacomo, Iago and James, each of them the same old name worn down differently by the language that carried it.

In South Africa it stands on its own as a full first name, not just a nickname.

Kennedy

An Irish surname, anglicised from Ó Cinnéide, meaning descendant of Cinnéidigh. The old personal name joins ceann, head, with éidigh, and has been glossed over the centuries as both helmeted head and, less flatteringly, ugly head.

The family took its name from a nephew of Brian Boru, High King of Ireland, in the tenth century.

Put together, the name crosses two seas: a Dutch name that settled at the Cape, and an Irish one that left Munster. Neither half was born in English, and both are now read in it.

XII K

A letter neither man needed

Two K's open this name. Giambattista Bodoni, whose face sets every word of it, spent his working life in a language that has almost no use for the letter at all.

The letter that dropped out

K reached Latin from Greek kappa, itself borrowed from a Semitic kaph, and was used early on, mostly before the vowel A. Classical Latin then let it go. C took over almost everywhere, and K survived only in a handful of fossils, chief among them Kalendae, the first day of the Roman month, the source of the English word calendar. The old praenomen Kaeso kept its K in the standard abbreviation “K.” long after most Romans had stopped writing the letter at all.

A foreign letter, still

Standard Italian carries the demotion further. Its native alphabet has 21 letters. J, K, W, X and Y are officially lettere straniere, foreign letters, kept only for loanwords, foreign names and technical terms: karate, kayak, never a native Italian word. A kilogram is a chilogrammo, a kilometre a chilometro, spelled with ch rather than imported whole. Kobus is Dutch by way of Afrikaans and Kennedy is Irish by way of Ó Cinnéide, and both of those languages use K as a plain working letter, not a borrowed one. Set in a face descended from an Italian printer's founts, the two K's in this name are foreigners twice over: to Bodoni's own alphabet, and now to the digital revival that carries his name.

What Bodoni himself cut

Giambattista Bodoni, 1740 to 1813, ran the Duke of Parma's royal press, the Stamperia Reale, from 1768 until his death. His two great type specimen books, the Manuale Tipografico of 1788 and the far larger edition his widow, Margherita Dall'Aglio, and his foreman, Luigi Orsi, published five years after he died, in 1818, are still called among the greatest specimen books ever printed. Neither shows a complete alphabet at every size the book contains; the 1788 Manuale drops the lowercase g outright above a certain point size. The 1818 edition goes beyond plain Italian roman altogether, with a German section in Gothic type and, in library records, a section of “caratteri inglesi,” English characters, though that same phrase was the standard Italian term for English roundhand cursive script, so it may describe a hand rather than a language's alphabet. Whether Bodoni's own working Italian roman founts, the type this whole site descends from, included a capital K at all, and what it looked like if they did, could not be found anywhere searched. Type specimens of the period conventionally displayed full founts rather than nation-bound alphabets, which argues a K existed somewhere in his cases. No source shows it.

Every revival needs one anyway

Every later Bodoni revival serves a market with no shortage of K's, so all of them have one: Morris Fuller Benton's ATF Bodoni, cut for American Type Founders between 1907 and 1911; Heinrich Jost's Bauer Bodoni, 1926 to 1927; Günter Gerhard Lange's Berthold Bodoni Old Face, 1983; the three-optical-size ITC Bodoni of 1994, drawn by a team led by Sumner Stone; Zuzana Licko's Filosofia, 1996, worked up from memory and synthesis rather than copied from one source; Nick Shinn's deliberately revisionist Bodoni Egyptian, 1999, revised 2010. Nothing found says how any of them approached K specifically, without an eighteenth-century Bodoni model to draw from. Licko has written about the gap in general terms: “There are many details in my revivals without a historical origin, such as the rounded terminals of the lower case ‘s,’ in Filosofia, which was a design decision within the context of the rounded serifs which I chose for Filosofia.” Whether the same is true of every revival's K, this site's own K included, is an inference this page is making, not a claim any source states.

The K on this page

This site is set in Bodoni Moda, designed by Owen Earl of the one-person foundry indestructible type*. Work began in January 2019; a 6-point optical size and variable-font builds followed in November 2019, small caps and math symbols in May 2020, redrawn italics in September 2020, and a build mastered specifically for Google Fonts in November 2020, released under the SIL Open Font License. The project's own log states its aim plainly: “Despite the tremendous popularity of Bodoni typefaces, there is currently no digital family without compromises. Bodoni* hopes to change that by being the first ever no-compromises Bodoni family, built for the digital age,” and its own description of its source is one line: “It is inspired by the designs of Giambattista Bodoni.” Nothing longer than that has been found. No interview, design note or specimen text describes how Owen Earl drew the letters Bodoni's own Italian founts never needed, K among them.
