Vol. III · The Presenter

X Presenter

Rafe Lavine

The man reading the names. Three decades of South African radio across five stations, a rock specialist who also ran the country's best-loved jazz hour, and the voice on this tape.

Trade
Radio presenter, 1983 to 2014, then voice artist
Stations
Radio 702 · Radio 5 · SAfm · Radio Today · Talk Radio 702
Specialism
Classic and progressive rock, with a sideline in jazz and soul
The voice
South African, warm, conversational, built for documentary
Company
Leon Economides, Chris Prior, David Blood, Phil Wright
Now
Dawlish, on the south Devon coast, retired

Career, as far as it could be traced

  1. 1983Radio 702. Cuts his teeth on the independent station, three years old at the time, presenting a show called Rock ’n Horror.
  2. 1984Radio 5, SABC. Moves to the national pop station and presents Hot Dog for six years, alongside a roster that included Alex Jay, Kevin Savage and Chris Prior.
  3. 1987SABC TV1. Appears on Pop Shop, the television pop programme, as one of the Radio 5 DJs. The clip survives on YouTube.
  4. 1990Off air. His Radio 5 contract ends. He manages music stores, then a music distribution company, and by his own account even tries his hand at selling assurance.
  5. 1990sSAfm. Back at the microphone on the SABC's English talk and music station with Rock Classics and the Great Unknowns, where he first shares a studio with Leon Economides, and The Quiet Storm, jazz and soul on Sunday nights from nine until midnight. The mystery-song feature on this tape, and Kobus Kennedy's call, belong to his SAfm years.
  6. 2003The Rock IT Scientists. Moved to SAfm's late slot, ten at night until one, he rings Economides and proposes a Friday co-presented show on the Rock Classics model: progressive, hard and classic rock, blues, brass rock, jazz and metal. “Rafe is the ultimate professional and a pleasure to work with,” Economides says a year in. “I really consider myself lucky.”
  7. 2004Axed. In the last week of September the SABC cuts almost all of SAfm's weekend programmes. The Quiet Storm is heard for the last time.
  8. 2006The RockFest. With Prior, Blood, Wright and Economides he founds a block of rock programming on the community station Radio Today, 1485 AM. It lasts about three and a half years, until the station stops. When RockFest resurfaces on Mix 93.8 FM in November 2009, he is not among the two who carry on.
  9. 2010sTalk Radio 702, again. Back where he began, presenting the weekend Solid Gold slot from four to seven in the evening.
  10. 2014Leaves South Africa. In December he signs off from Solid Gold to join his family in the United Kingdom.
  11. NowDawlish, Devon. Retired from radio after almost thirty years. Listed as a voice artist for commercial and documentary work, with the accent he took with him.
Rock music around the world, but particularly in this country over the last couple of years, has been neglected. There is a huge market out there, a more mature and affluent market that has not been catered for. Rafe Lavine, March 2004
The umbrella under which I do my show is entertainment. Many of the younger people out there are getting tired of listening to artists that are just giving them re-worked versions of classic songs, and they are interested in where the original material comes from. Rafe Lavine, March 2004
Leon is a certified lunatic. The amount of money he has spent on music over the years is incredible, but he has always had a passion for music and it's always been quality. Rafe Lavine on Leon Economides, his co-presenter
His shows were like an institution. Very informative, and you could tell he did his research and knew much more about the music. Concord Nkabinde, bassist, on The Quiet Storm

Ten records, in his own order

Asked in 2004 for the albums that made him, Lavine gave this list, with the remarks that follow each one. Seven of the ten are from the same decade as the mystery song, and one of them is the record that got him into jazz.

  1. 1Led Zeppelin IV 1971“A life-changing experience. When Black Dog leapt out of the speakers, I couldn't believe what I was hearing. A benchmark for hard rock.”
  2. 2Quincy Jones Smackwater Jack 1971“A ground-breaking album. Funk, jazz, rock and Q's legendary arranging skills fuse into a must-have album. The extended version of Marvin Gaye's What's Going On is a landmark.”
  3. 3AC/DC Highway to Hell 1979“No-nonsense, down-to-earth hard, grinding, tough boogie. And Bon Scott's vocals cut through armourplate!”
  4. 4The Peddlers Three in a Cell 1968“Unsung heroes, this trio of Brit musicians. Have something of a cult following here in SA. This was the album that got me into jazz in a big way.”
  5. 5Donald Fagen The Nightfly 1982“The Dan's wry irony transferred seamlessly into this slick, sassy, rootsy milestone.”
  6. 6Pink Floyd The Dark Side of the Moon 1973“Words are superfluous!”
  7. 7Greatest Show on Earth The Going's Easy 1970“A classic example of why Brit prog rock is so highly regarded, with a razor sharp horn section. An album one never tires of listening to.”
  8. 8Livin' Blues Blue Breeze 1975“From Holland. THE blues-rock album of the 20th century. Passion, fire, evocative songs. Pure Nirvana!”
  9. 9Maynard Ferguson MF Horn 2 1972“A horn section that will simply blow your head off, if you can find it. Not your head, the album!”
  10. 10Dream Theater Metropolis Pt. 2: Scenes from a Memory 1999“The ultimate concept album. Mind-boggling arrangements, stupefying musicianship and a chilling tale, with a South African connection: Kevin ‘Caveman’ Shirley.”

Not one of the ten is Pick Up the Pieces. But a man whose second-favourite record is Quincy Jones fusing funk, jazz and rock, and whose ninth is a big band with a horn section that blows your head off, is exactly the man to hand a nine-second Average White Band riff to a country and dare it to name the tune.

His SAfm programmes

Four can be documented. The tape's “this afternoon” fits none of them, so the mystery-song feature most likely ran on an earlier daytime programme from his first SAfm years, before the late slot.

  1. Rock Classics and the Great Unknowns1990sThe show on which he first worked with Leon Economides. Classic rock alongside records nobody else would play. Its format was revived in 2003 as the Rock IT Scientists.
  2. The Quiet StormSundays, 21:00 to midnightJazz, soul and smooth late-night music, the programme listeners remember him for most. Axed in the SABC's weekend cull, last heard in September 2004. A smooth-jazz compilation CD bearing the programme's name and his was later circulated, though no catalogue entry for it could be found.
  3. The late slotWeeknights, 22:00 to 01:00The 2003 radio guide lists it simply as “Rafe Lavine”, with no programme title. Three hours of music five nights a week, which is where the Friday rock show lived.
  4. The Rock IT ScientistsFridays, from 2003Co-presented with Economides: progressive, hard and classic rock, blues, brass rock, Latin and African rock, jazz and metal. The show Lavine described as coming under “the umbrella of entertainment”.

XIII The Peddlers

Unsung heroes, apparently

Chapter X already carries the line. Asked in 2004 for the ten records that made him, Rafe Lavine named a 1968 album by a trio he called “unsung heroes,” with, he said, “something of a cult following here in SA.” This chapter follows that trio, and tests that one claim.

Formed
Manchester, April 1964, first billed as Song Peddlers
Trio
Roy Phillips, Tab Martin, Trevor Morais
Labels
Philips to 1967, CBS from 1967, Philips again from 1970
Best-charting single
Girlie, No. 1, New Zealand, May 1970
UK best
Birth, No. 17, September 1969
Split
Reported as 1976, on one account only

Out of a Joe Meek session

Roy Phillips started on piano at five and was sitting in with Bournemouth pub bands by twelve. As a teenager he played guitar in a group called the Soundtracks, backing a duo billed as the Dowland Brothers, and it was there that the producer Joe Meek heard him and signed him on as a session guitarist in the spring of 1963. When Heinz Burt left the Tornados that year, Meek built a new band, the Saints, to back him, with Phillips on guitar and a bassist named Tab Martin. The Saints cut several singles for Meek and broke up in April 1964, the same month Phillips, Martin and a Liverpool drummer named Trevor Morais formed a trio of their own in Manchester.

Phillips gave up the guitar for the Hammond organ around the same time, by his own account because the session player next to him at Meek's already had the guitar covered.

I was rat-shit at it. I could never improve on what I did… My buddy in session work in those days was Ritchie Blackmore, he used to do all the fancy stuff. Roy Phillips, Record Collector, “Under the Radar”

The organ itself came from a shop that could not sell it.

The owner of the shop told me he couldn't get rid of it, so he said if I could get any use out of it with the new group I could have it. So, it got to be the lead instrument for the instrumental trio. Roy Phillips, Record Collector, “Under the Radar”

Eighteen months at the Pickwick

The new trio's first booking, a Manchester cabaret night with Jimmy Savile as compere, went badly.

I was thrown into exerting lung power, but I did not intend to ever be a singer, I just wanted to be a muso keyboard player. Our first gig was a disaster, we cleared the dancefloor at the CIS tower in Manchester with Jimmy Savile as compere. A great start, eh? Roy Phillips, Record Collector, “Under the Radar”

London went better. A one-month residency at the Pickwick Club, on Great Newport Street, stretched to around eighteen months; Frank Sinatra reportedly caught the act and recommended the trio for the Flamingo in Las Vegas; and Philips signed them. Six singles and an EP followed on that label between 1964 and 1967, along with a live album taped at the Pickwick itself, before the group moved to CBS in 1967.

Three in a Cell

The album Lavine named, the trio's second, is Three in a Cell, CBS S 63411, 1968: a set of standards (On a Clear Day You Can See Forever, People, Basin Street Blues, Ebb Tide among them) and a handful of originals, played by a Hammond-led trio with, on some tracks, string arrangements by Keith Mansfield. What the title refers to is not settled anywhere searched, whether it is wordplay on three musicians confined to one small combo, or a literal cell pictured on the sleeve; no critic or band member is on record saying either way. The producer is given as Mervyn Conn in most places, but who engineered the record is reported two different ways, and a co-producer credit for a Cyril Smith turns up on one reissue listing without corroboration elsewhere. Even the track count will not settle: some listings give ten tracks, others eleven, with an extra closing song, Prime of My Life, on the longer version.

Birth, Girlie, and New Zealand

The trio's two best-known singles came the following year, both written by Phillips for CBS. Birth reached No. 17 in the UK in September 1969; Girlie reached No. 34 in January 1970. Girlie did far better somewhere else. In New Zealand it went to No. 1 in May 1970, the trio's highest chart placing anywhere, and they followed it with a tour reported at around twelve weeks.

We played every place imaginable and thoroughly loved it. Roy Phillips, RNZ, “Roy Phillips still going strong 50 years after ‘Girlie’”

After the trio

Trevor Morais left during an Australian tour in 1972, and a New Zealander, Paul Johnston, replaced him. One account, uncorroborated elsewhere, puts the band's end in 1976. Morais had drummed for Rory Storm and the Hurricanes before the Peddlers, replacing Ringo Starr when Starr left for the Beatles in 1962; afterward he joined Quantum Jump, whose “The Lone Ranger” became Kenny Everett's television theme and reached No. 5 on re-release in 1979, then worked as a touring and session drummer for artists including Björk, recording her 1996 album Homogenic partly at his own studio in Málaga. Whether he is still living could not be confirmed either way.

Tab Martin went into session work and later moved to Portugal. Nothing further about him, including whether he is alive, could be found anywhere searched.

Roy Phillips emigrated to New Zealand in 1981, ran a restaurant, wrote more than two hundred songs including the New Zealand Olympic team's 1996 anthem “I Have a Dream,” and kept recording at a home studio in Christchurch he called the Groove Room, his last album released in 2019. He died at home in Christchurch on 24 April 2025, aged eighty-three, after some years of emphysema. His own account of the Hammond he had played since Manchester:

It was my baby for many, many years. In the end that organ of mine was held together by gaffer tape, it was smashed, dropped and goodness knows what, and still played. Roy Phillips, RNZ, “Roy Phillips still going strong 50 years after ‘Girlie’”

The cult following, examined

Set against all that, South Africa is close to silent. No chart entry for the band or either single turned up on the LM Radio Hit Parade or the Springbok Radio Preservation Society's own listings. No South African pressing of any Peddlers record appears on Discogs or 45cat, only British, American and New Zealand ones. No tour date, review or gig listing for the band in South Africa turned up anywhere searched, and no interview with any of the three musicians mentions the country at all. Every search built from Lavine's own wording returned either his 2004 profile itself or content unrelated to it.

Measured the same way, New Zealand is the opposite case: a No. 1 single, a lengthy tour, a reunion show in Auckland in 2009, and a member who emigrated there and stayed the rest of his life. If the trio kept a following on the far side of the world from Manchester, the documented one is that one, not South Africa's. None of which disproves Lavine's memory. It says only that nobody else, so far searched, has written it down.

Roy Phillips built the trio's whole sound around one instrument, an organ so battered by the end, he said, that gaffer tape held it together. A Hammond's voice comes from nine drawbars, each one a harmonic of the note played, pulled out to mix more of that harmonic in or pushed in to silence it.
