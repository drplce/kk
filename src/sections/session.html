Two thousand miles for two songs

How a Scottish band on a dropped contract ended up in a Miami studio with an American producer, and what happened there, on both sides of the microphone.

Off MCA, onto Atlantic

The band's first album, Show Your Hand, came out on MCA in 1973. It earned a television slot on the BBC's Old Grey Whistle Test and warm reviews, and sold poorly enough that MCA dropped them. What saved the band was a party in Laurel Canyon, Los Angeles, at the house of a music-industry friend named Alan Pariser. Atlantic's Jerry Wexler was there and heard a tape.

I heard them for the first time at my friend Alan Pariser's Laurel Canyon enclave of hi-fi equipment and high-octane fun. I walked in and couldn't believe what I was seeing and hearing… Their funk hit me where I lived, their tape was great, and I wanted to sign them on the spot. Jerry Wexler, reportedly from his memoir Rhythm and the Blues, quoted secondhand

Wexler signed them and handed them to Arif Mardin, who at the time was in Miami producing Aretha Franklin. That is the plain reason a band of Scots ended up recording in Florida.

Three studios for one song

The album that became AWB was not cut in one room. The band tracked the bulk of it at Clover Studios in Hollywood, then Mardin brought them to Atlantic's usual Miami room, Criteria Studios, to record two more songs he wanted, “Nothing You Can Do” and “You Got It.” What came back from Miami sounded different enough that Atlantic changed the plan again.

The sound quality was so much better than the work we'd done in Hollywood that Atlantic decided to fly us to New York to re-record seven tracks in their Manhattan studios. Onnie McIntyre, reported in Mix magazine's Classic Tracks

Criteria itself had a pedigree to justify the detour. Mack Emerman founded it in North Miami in 1958 and built its reputation to the point where, through his friendship with Atlantic engineer Tom Dowd, it became known as Atlantic Records South. James Brown cut “I Got You (I Feel Good)” there in 1965, the studio's first gold record.

Between 1967 and 1975, you couldn't get into Criteria with a shoehorn. Tom Dowd, on the studio's busiest years

The AWB sessions ran through the same Miami spring as Eric Clapton's 461 Ocean Boulevard, cut at Criteria in April and May 1974. Two records that define a certain 1974 came out of the same building within weeks of each other.

The Dundee horns

The riff belongs to Roger Ball, alto saxophone and keyboards, who wrote it and plays the alto half of the unison horn line. The tenor beside him is Malcolm “Molly” Duncan. The two met at Duncan of Jordanstone College of Art in Dundee and are sometimes called the Dundee horns, a nickname one source credits to the singer Maggie Bell, though that origin is not independently confirmed.

By Duncan's own later account, the band recorded the song on a Friday, all but the solo. Mardin had not decided whether the solo should go to alto or tenor, and called Ball and Duncan back in on the Saturday morning to settle it. Mardin's ear went to the tenor. The solo on the record is Duncan's.

The songwriting credit is split six ways, Ball, Stuart, Gorrie, McIntyre, McIntosh and Duncan, the whole band. Duncan died on 8 October 2019, aged 74, in Germany. Nothing dated has turned up on Ball's life after he left the band in 1997.

Without their drummer

Robbie McIntosh died in Los Angeles, most sources say on 23 September 1974, one source gives the 22nd, after a party following a Troubadour show. He and Alan Gorrie are reported to have taken what they believed was cocaine and was in fact heroin. Gorrie survived. The party's host, named in accounts as a 30-year-old, Kenneth Moss, was indicted for murder by a grand jury and later pleaded guilty to involuntary manslaughter.

Steve Ferrone, a friend of McIntosh's who had been watching the band's Los Angeles shows that week, joined within days.

Immediately everybody felt that if we break up now it would be a waste of McIntosh's life and all his work, to just get this far and then buckle under just because that happened. Alan Gorrie, reported in Wax Poetics
It was an absolutely automatic choice. He was the only guy that could do the gig. Alan Gorrie, on hiring Steve Ferrone

“Pick Up the Pieces” reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 on 22 February 1975, the same week the album AWB topped the album chart, an instrumental single and its LP both at number one at once. The band's next album, Cut the Cake, released in 1975, is dedicated to their friend and brother Robbie McIntosh. He is buried at Barnhill Cemetery, Dundee.
