XIV Kobus

From a heel to five letters

Chapter I gave the first name two lines: a short form of Jakobus, the Dutch Jacob. This chapter follows the name along its chain, from Hebrew to Greek to Latin to Dutch to Afrikaans, and asks who carries it now.

A heel, or a protection

The book of Genesis gives the name its own explanation. The twin who is born second comes out holding his brother's heel, and the verse explains the name from that. One English translation, the Complete Jewish Bible, renders it this way.

Then his brother emerged, with his hand holding ‘Esav's heel, so he was called Ya'akov [he catches by the heel, he supplants]. Genesis 25:26, Complete Jewish Bible, as shown in a search extract

Modern summaries call that a folk etymology. The same summaries say the historical origin of the name is uncertain, and mention a proposed older form, Yaʿaqovʾel, which would mean “may God protect.” So the heel is the Bible's account of the name, and the protection is a scholar's guess. Which one the name started as is left open here.

Two roads from the same Latin

Greek Iákōbos became Latin Iacobus. From there the name splits. In the Romance languages the spoken form is said to have drifted to Vulgar Latin Iacomus, which gave Old French James and, in English, James. Wiktionary, in a search extract, lists as doublets of that English name Jacques, Jacob, Iago, Santiago, Tiago and Yaakov, along with the Scots Hamish and Irish Seamus. The Dutch road kept the Latin shape, Jacobus, and wore it down by cutting the front off.

One caution from the research: Diego, often listed beside Santiago, is not Saint James. San Diego is Saint Didacus of Alcalá, and the link to James is described as a folk etymology of a couple of centuries' standing.

Cut down, not shrunk

The Wiktionary appendix of Dutch short forms of given names lists, for Jacobus, a long run that includes Jaap, Jaco, Japie, Joop, Kobes, Kobus, Kok, Kokkie and Koos. Kobus is one of dozens of ways a Dutch speaker cut the name down. Wikipedia describes Kobus as a short form of Jacobus, now primarily Afrikaans. Whether it counts as a diminutive is disputed in the material found: one source says it is not, because the usual Dutch diminutive endings, -je and -ke, are missing. That is a reasoned claim from a search extract and is recorded as open.

The count on the instrument is plain arithmetic: Jacobus has seven letters, Kobus five. The front syllable Ja- went, and the hard K stands where the J and C had been. That is the whole of the change, and it is why this name needs a K, twice over, at the head of a name that Chapter XII found foreign to its typeface.

Named for a grandfather

Two sources, both seen only as search extracts, describe a naming convention that would keep a name like Jakobus in circulation. In the Dutch pattern the first son was named after his father's father and the second after his mother's father. A paper on Afrikaner naming says the same, and adds that the third son took the father's name. A study of first names in the Eastern Cape found, per its summary, that more than one in three first-born children were named for a family member, and that boys were more than twice as likely as girls to get such a name. Nothing found ties those customs to Kobus in particular, or dates the name's arrival at the Cape.

Not the antelope

A different Kobus sits in the zoology books. Kobus is a genus of six antelope species in the family Bovidae, established by Smith in 1840: the kob, the waterbuck, the lechwe, the Nile lechwe, the puku and the Upemba lechwe. The genus name is said to be a Neo-Latin word from an African name, koba. No source found links it to the given name, which is a negative finding and not proof. There is also a surname Kobus, which one extract says is a short form of Jakobus in several European languages and in Polish a nickname from a word for falcon.
