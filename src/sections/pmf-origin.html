The name

It is thought that Pigman was a sort of nickname, earned by his appearance and manners and customs; but Fat was his surname. DTW, “The Battle of the airwaves”

So the name in this volume’s title is a nickname and a surname, in that order, and nothing in the original is a pig that gained weight. The essay says the surname is pronounced Fett. That is also how the Afrikaans word vet, fat, is said, but the essay does not say the surname comes from it, and no source was found. The note is this site’s own.

Copying the quizzes

The essay is about the struggle for a shrinking pool of radio listeners and sponsors, played out behind the scenes and on the shows themselves. Fat, it says, launched rival quiz programmes that blatantly copied and changed the originals. A programme called 21 had ended, so he got the rights to name his show the Skip show, then went one better and called it Skip show 22. The rules were the same as 21, but the quizmaster interpreted them to his liking. A quick route to 22 was two scores of 11, and the quizmaster would sometimes accept a very approximate answer and grant 11 points. The essay says O’Bryne and Shippey, whom it does not otherwise identify, would never have considered doing that. The listeners’ “missing answer” question was never won.

When that show ended, he introduced Find the Job, which the essay calls a clone of Just the Job, where a contestant has 20 questions to identify someone’s occupation. Like 22, it is described as riddled with inconsistency and flexible rules, with jobs changed at will. It became, the essay says, a minor hit.

Venture, and a hat at half time

Another quiz, Venture, was running, and a man the essay names as Mik Boaty started his own version of it. The essay says it was short of cash from the start. Contestants who ended on a negative score had to pay the debt on leaving, and Boaty added a category called Unlucky Dip, in which a contestant immediately lost the amount nominated.

Then Fat launched a take-over bid for the programme. He secured sponsorship from what the essay calls a huge, popular soft drink manufacturer, and launched his own version of Venture, which the essay says absorbed the sponsorship that Boaty’s version had. Boaty responded by sending his scorer, named in the essay as Le’vil Causson, into the audience with a hat at half time to take a collection, with a running commentary to coax the audience to give more. None of these programmes charged an admission fee. There was no room for so many ventures, and Boaty’s collapsed. The essay sympathises with Boaty, and also says one should not feel overly sorry for him, given the Unlucky Dip.

The last of the radio quizzes

As television took up more of the sponsors’ time and money, the essay says, Fat made a defiant attempt to keep the radio quizzes going. It describes an autocrat who answered to nobody and ran everything himself, an enigma with no interviews on record, ruthless in building up his repertoire and ready to change the format to suit the times. He eventually ran out of sponsorship and closed down. The essay adds that there was already uncertainty about the future of radio, and that many stations later changed hands. Of the programmes, it says, nothing is left but memories.

What listeners wrote

The essay says letters to the newspapers at the time showed mixed views, with only a few supporting his methods. As the essay reports them, the complaints were that he showed favouritism and was openly biased, that he put the sponsors first and gave them excessive airtime, that his presentation style was sinister, and that his competitions could never be won, one letter-writer claiming they were rigged because the answers were incorrect. The essay answers that it was a one-man show with no producer or auditor to check the questions and answers, and that the critics might try running a programme in a deteriorating environment with no resources. These are complaints as the essay relays them. This site has seen none of the letters.

Why the name lasted

Pigman Fat (or PMF as he is sometimes known), has become famous, some would say notorious. His moniker is also a by-word for piggishness. DTW, “The Battle of the airwaves”

The essay closes by wondering why his memory lives on, and offers two answers: he was probably “larger than life” and memorable, and nostalgia could play a part. Chapters XV and XVI make up a different story about the same name, and nothing in them was taken from this essay. Chapter IX is about another radio game, Beat the Beat. Nothing found connects the two.
