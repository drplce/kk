I Origins

Kobus

An Afrikaans given name, the short form of Jakobus, which is the Dutch form of Jacob. It sits in the same family as Jacques, Giacomo, Iago and James, each of them the same old name worn down differently by the language that carried it.

In South Africa it stands on its own as a full first name, not just a nickname.

Kennedy

An Irish surname, anglicised from Ó Cinnéide, meaning descendant of Cinnéidigh. The old personal name joins ceann, head, with éidigh, and has been glossed over the centuries as both helmeted head and, less flatteringly, ugly head.

The family took its name from a nephew of Brian Boru, High King of Ireland, in the tenth century.

Put together, the name crosses two seas: a Dutch name that settled at the Cape, and an Irish one that left Munster. Neither half was born in English, and both are now read in it.

IX Broadcast

Nine seconds and a riddle

A recording of Beat the Beat, the SABC radio show that plays a sliver of a record and asks the country to name it. Kobus Kennedy phoned in and did.

Pick Up the Pieces
Average White Band

The riddle points one way. Gather up the shattered remnants: pick up the pieces. Your super-ego: the pieces are yours. And in 1974 the record that shouts exactly that, once, over a horn riff everyone knows by the second bar, is the Average White Band's.

Six Scots playing American funk so convincingly that Atlantic Records signed them and Arif Mardin produced them. The track is an instrumental save for the one shouted title, and the sliver you hear at eighteen seconds is enough. A nine-second identikit was generous.

“Did you gather up the shattered remnants of your super-ego if you got beat yesterday?”

The presenter's clue does two jobs. It paraphrases the title without using a word of it, and it winks at the show's own name: to get beat is to lose at Beat the Beat. Anyone who lost yesterday was being told, gently, to pick up the pieces.

The record itself has a harder story. Drummer Robbie McIntosh, one of the six credited writers, died in Los Angeles in September 1974, weeks before the single began its climb. The band picked up the pieces and the song went to number one without him.

Six callers, one name

The presenter reads the winners in the order they rang. Two from Cape Town phoned for the first time and both got it, and one of them is the subject of this page. First-time callers are called out on air as a small honour, and the name is read clearly, twice qualified: also Cape Town, also a first-time caller.

Whisper-class speech recognition heard the name as “Corbis Kennedy”. Human ears, and the man who was there, know better.

Beat the Beat

A South African radio quiz on the SABC. The format in this clip is the whole format: a year, a nine-second identikit of the record, and a cryptic clue read once. Listeners telephone the studio with the title, and the presenter reads the roll of those who got it.

Forty seconds of tape carry the year, the clue, the sliver of the song and six names. Nothing is wasted, which is what live radio sounded like when a call meant a landline and a wait.
